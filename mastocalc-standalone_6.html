Learn: breast cancer, screening and staying aware

General education, not medical advice. Screening guidance below reflects major US organizations as of October 2026 and does change, so confirm with your clinician.

About 1 in 8

US women will be diagnosed with breast cancer at some point in their lives. It is the most common cancer in women after skin cancer.

~99% vs ~1 in 3

Five-year survival is about 99% when breast cancer is found while confined to the breast, versus roughly 1 in 3 once it has spread to distant organs.

Most have no family history

The majority of women diagnosed have no close relative with the disease, so being "low risk" on paper is not a reason to skip screening.

Men too

About 1% of cases occur in men. Everyone has breast tissue, and the same awareness applies.

What is breast cancer?

Breast cancer starts when cells in breast tissue, most often the milk ducts or lobules, grow out of control and can form a tumor. Some cancers stay in place (in situ, such as DCIS); others invade nearby tissue and can travel through lymph nodes or blood to other parts of the body. There are several subtypes, defined mainly by whether the cells carry hormone receptors or HER2, and they behave and are treated differently. Many are found early and are highly treatable.

Why mammograms matter

A mammogram is a low-dose X-ray of the breast. It can reveal cancers years before they are big enough to feel, when treatment is usually smaller, simpler and more successful. Finding cancer at an early stage is the main reason survival is so much higher for screened populations.

Screening is aimed at people with no symptoms. Waiting until you notice something means the cancer has often grown. Regular mammograms also give your radiologist older images to compare against, which helps spot small changes.

What to expect at a mammogram

The visit usually takes about 20 minutes. You undress from the waist up, and a technologist positions each breast between two plates that press it flat for a few seconds. This can feel uncomfortable but should not be very painful, and tender breasts are easier to image a week away from your period. Skip deodorant or powder that day, and bring or request prior images. Many sites now offer 3D mammography (tomosynthesis). Results arrive by letter or portal, often within a couple of weeks.

Being called back for more pictures is common and does not mean you have cancer. Most callbacks turn out to be normal or benign.

Honest limits of mammography

Mammograms are the best proven screening tool but are not perfect. They can miss some cancers, especially in dense breasts. They can cause false alarms that lead to extra tests and worry, and they occasionally find slow-growing cancers that might never have caused harm (overdiagnosis). The radiation dose is small, about like several weeks of natural background radiation. Most experts conclude that the benefit of finding cancers early outweighs these downsides for the recommended ages.

Dense breasts and higher-risk situations

Roughly half of women over 40 have dense breasts, meaning more glandular and fibrous tissue than fat. Density slightly raises cancer risk and can hide tumors on a mammogram. US mammogram reports now include a notice about your density. If you are told you have dense breasts, ask whether extra imaging such as ultrasound or MRI makes sense for you.

Higher-risk groups include people with a known BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, PALB2 or similar variant, a strong family history (several relatives, early onset, ovarian or male breast cancer), chest radiation before about age 30, or a calculated lifetime risk of roughly 20% or more. They are often advised to start earlier, often in the late 20s to 30, and to alternate or combine mammograms with breast MRI. A genetic counselor can help decide whether testing is worthwhile.

What you can do to lower your risk

No habit guarantees protection, and many cases happen in people with no risk factors. Still, several choices help:

  • Limit alcohol. Risk rises with each drink per week; less is better, and none is lowest.
  • Maintain a healthy weight, especially after menopause, and avoid weight gain in adulthood.
  • Be active. Aim for around 150 minutes a week of moderate activity.
  • Do not smoke.
  • Breastfeed if you can. Longer total breastfeeding is modestly protective.
  • Review hormone therapy with your doctor. Combined estrogen plus progestin raises risk with longer use; use the lowest dose for the shortest time that you need.
  • Know your family history and update your doctor when it changes. Ask about genetic counseling if it is strong.
  • Ask about risk-reducing medicines (such as tamoxifen, raloxifene or aromatase inhibitors) or surgery if you are at high risk. These are specialist decisions with real trade-offs.
  • Keep up with screening. It does not prevent cancer but finds it when it is easiest to treat.
Staying aware: changes to watch for

Get familiar with how your breasts normally look and feel so you notice what is new. Formal monthly self-exams are optional, but awareness of change matters. See a clinician promptly for:

  • A new lump or thickened area in the breast or armpit
  • Change in size, shape or contour of a breast
  • Dimpling, puckering or skin that looks like an orange peel
  • Redness, scaling or flaking of the skin or nipple
  • A nipple that turns inward, or discharge that appears on its own, especially if bloody
  • Persistent pain in one spot that does not go away
Most breast lumps are not cancer, but do not wait to find out. A normal mammogram does not rule out a new lump, so get new symptoms checked even if you were recently screened.
Common myths
  • "No family history means no risk." False. Most people diagnosed have no affected close relative.
  • "A lump means cancer." False. Most lumps are benign cysts or other harmless changes, but they still need evaluation.
  • "Mammograms cause cancer." The radiation dose is very small and the benefit of early detection outweighs it at recommended ages.
  • "Only older women get it." Risk rises with age, but cases occur in younger women too, which is why guidelines have been moving earlier.
  • "Antiperspirants or underwire bras cause it." Large reviews have not shown a clear link.

Frequently asked questions

What is MastoCalc?

An experimental, educational risk model that combines many breast-cancer risk factors with weights, interactions and age-specific population incidence. It is a software prototype, not a clinical tool.

How accurate is it?

It is not validated. Coefficients are approximate, literature-inspired values chosen by hand, not fitted to patient data, so outputs can be materially wrong. Clinicians use validated tools such as the NCI Breast Cancer Risk Assessment Tool (Gail), IBIS/Tyrer-Cuzick and CanRisk (BOADICEA).

Relative vs. absolute risk?

Relative risk (×1.5) compares you with an average person of your age and sex. Absolute risk (e.g. 8%) is the chance of developing cancer within a time window. Absolute risk applies the relative risk to age-specific incidence and accounts for the chance of dying of other causes first.

What if I don’t know a value?

Leave it blank. Blank fields are treated as population-average and contribute nothing, but the uncertainty range widens and the completeness meter drops.

What is a z-score?

How many standard deviations a measurement is from a reference population’s average. 0 = average, +2 = unusually high (top ~2%), −2 = unusually low. Lab or research reports usually supply it; otherwise (value − reference mean) ÷ reference SD.

What are the experimental markers?

Circulating proteins, cfDNA methylation/fragmentation and metabolomic signatures are active research areas but are not validated for risk prediction. They are labeled, heavily shrunk, capped, and reported separately so they never silently change the core result.

Why are race and ancestry included?

Baseline incidence differs between populations, and some risk-model calibrations differ. They are social/statistical proxies, carry the smallest weights, and matter less when genetic data is entered. Subgroup options are optional and their effects are very small and uncertain.

What does "To age 90" mean?

The cumulative chance of breast cancer from your current age to 90, allowing for competing mortality. Some guidelines use a lifetime risk of ≥20% and a 5-year risk of ≥1.67% (age ≥35) as flags for extra discussion with a clinician.

Does my data leave my device?

No. The calculator makes no network requests. Use the export buttons to save a report or JSON file locally.

I’m worried about my risk.

Please speak with a physician or genetic counselor. They can use validated tools, family-history review and, where appropriate, genetic testing and screening plans.
